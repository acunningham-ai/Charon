Coming next · the marquee

A harness that reviews its own failures

When an automation breaks, Charon will read the wreck for you — the failure, the recent runs, the command that broke — and hand you a ranked set of fixes. It surfaces and stops. You pick; a separate, gated step applies. It never quietly fixes itself.

01

Failure

A scheduled run exits with an error and logs it.

02

Auto-review

A hardened, read-only pass finds the likely root cause.

03

Ranked options

A decision-ready note: cause + fixes, most-likely first.

04

You pick

The human gate. Nothing has changed up to here.

05

Gated apply

A separate, allow-listed step makes the reversible fix.

06

Monitor

A deterministic check confirms the problem actually fell.

Why it's built this way. The review reads; it cannot act. The apply step is a closed allow-list of reversible fixes, each needing your say-so. The "did it work?" check answers from deterministic counts, not the model grading its own homework — so the loop can't talk itself into a win.

Just shipped

Four ways to get more out of your own vault — now live

These landed in the latest release. Small, read-only tools for the moments a growing second brain starts to strain — finding, reconciling, pressure-testing, and handing off.

/recallshipped

Find the note you half-remember

Hybrid keyword retrieval over everything you've authored — body and title ranked together — with zero setup and no vector database to stand up.

Outcome: the right note in one query, on a plain install.

/find-tensionsshipped

Catch where your vault disagrees with itself

Surfaces two notes asserting incompatible facts — a date, a number, a verdict — and records each pair side by side. It never averages and never picks a winner.

Outcome: stale contradictions caught before they reach a report.

/grillshipped

Pressure-test a plan against your own record

Interrogates a plan one question at a time, first checking it against what you've already decided — so you don't re-litigate settled ground or contradict a past call.

Outcome: a consistency check no fresh brainstorm can give you.

/handoffshipped

Put a thread down and pick it back up

Writes a dated handoff note and a paste-ready kickoff prompt for the next session, wired into your front-of-mind list.

Outcome: resume after days away without re-deriving where you were.

Coming next · a second way in

You just talk to it.

Everyone has the same picture in their head. The ship’s computer on the Enterprise, answering a question put to the room. Tony Stark talking to JARVIS with his hands busy on something else. Not typing into a box and waiting — asking, and having something that already knows your work answer you back.

That is what is being built here, and this is the first version of it. Hold a key, say what you want, and it arrives in the live session as though you had typed it. Not a voice menu bolted over a handful of commands — the same interface, so anything you can ask, you can ask aloud. The speech-to-text runs on your own machine, so nothing you say is sent anywhere to be heard by anyone.

And the reason it was worth building at all: every other capability on this site quietly assumes you can sit at a keyboard and type. For anyone who cannot type comfortably, or for long, that assumption is the difference between a system they can use and one they cannot. Reaching your notes while your hands are busy is the pleasant side effect — access is the point.

01

You hold a key

The keypress is the consent. There is no open microphone, at any point, by design.

02

You speak

Up to two minutes. A stuck key cannot open an endless recording.

03

It transcribes on your machine

Local speech-to-text. No audio is sent anywhere to be heard by anyone.

04

It checks where it is about to type

Two checks must both pass before a single character lands. Wrong window, or wrong tab: refused.

05

It arrives as text

In the session, submitted, exactly as if you had typed it yourself.

Why the face looks like this. Each state carries three independent signals — a colour, a distinct kind of motion, and the word itself, printed large. Colour alone would fail a colour-blind viewer. Words alone are slow to read across a room. Motion alone is ambiguous, which is why listening pushes rings outward while thinking orbits, rather than the two being fast and slow versions of one animation. Any one of the three carries the meaning on its own, so the state is readable by someone who has never seen it before, from a distance, or on a projector. That is the same reason the interface exists at all, applied to its own display.

the actual renderer, running here — driven by sample data rather than a live microphone

Why it’s built this way. Dictation types into a live terminal, and a terminal runs what it is given — that is the entire hazard, and it is why step 04 exists. Checking which program is in front is not enough: two tabs of the same terminal window are the same program, share the same window handle, and are indistinguishable by every obvious signal. The guard therefore also reads the window title, and both checks must pass. Tested against exactly that trap on a real machine — two tabs, identical process, identical handle, one running Claude and one running a shell. Only the title told them apart, and the shell was refused. A broken pattern degrades to refusing, never to allowing.

What it does not do yet. It is push-to-talk: it needs a key held down, so it is not hands-free, and for someone whose barrier is motor rather than linguistic that key is itself a barrier. Hands-free needs activation solved without an open microphone, which is an unsolved design question here rather than an unwritten feature — so it is not promised. Voice also cannot approve anything consequential; gated actions stay gated and come back to the keyboard. And with two Claude sessions open it can still type into the wrong one — visible, nothing executes, but real.

This is version 1. Everything above that it cannot do yet is a v1 boundary, not a ceiling — it grows as Charon does. It runs on Windows today; macOS and Linux are planned updates rather than conditions it has to meet before anyone can use it. The destination is the one everybody already pictures when they think of talking to a computer, the ship’s computer or JARVIS: you speak, and the whole system answers. Charon is not there. A grounded answer currently takes about eighty seconds, most of it fixed overhead rather than thinking, and saying otherwise would be the first dishonest sentence on this site. It is the direction, stated as a direction.

Coming next · the shift

It brings you the right notes. You don’t have to remember which.

A second brain usually leans on one list that loads every single time. Everything you must not forget competes for a place on it, so it only grows — and anything that isn’t on the list may as well not exist. Charon is moving to the opposite arrangement: ask a normal question, and the few notes that actually relate come to you — including ones you wrote months ago and had forgotten you had.

01

You just ask

A plain question. No file names, no commands, nothing to remember.

02

It reads the question

Picks out what you actually named — a project, a person, a decision.

03

It follows your links

Your notes already point at each other. It walks those connections.

04

Only what relates

A short list, a line each on what it says. Not everything you own.

05

The list stops mattering

Nothing has to earn a place on an index to be findable any more.

Why it’s built this way. Only notes you wrote are eligible. Captured email, calendar invites and meeting transcripts are deliberately excluded — anyone can send you a meeting invite, and content that arrives from outside should never quietly put words in front of you. That check is based on where a note came from rather than where it sits in a folder, and it fails safe: if it can’t tell, the note stays out. Nothing is ever dropped to save room either — when space is tight the detail shortens, never the list, because a note you can’t see is a note you don’t have.

Coming next · the quiet one

Things you promised yourself stop slipping.

Decide to revisit something in a fortnight and that date lives in a note, which means it survives only if you happen to reread it. Charon is gaining a small register for those: reviews, follow-ups, the “let’s check this again once it’s proven” commitments. They appear on your daily list and again when you sit down — and if a date passes, it reads as overdue, not as still-pending.

The same lifecycle tidies your read-first list. Anything finished, or quiet for a month, gets proposed for retirement — and anything unfinished is recorded before it leaves, so tidying up can never quietly lose a thread.

How anything gets here

Proven first, then shipped

Nothing lands in Charon until it's run for weeks on a real, working vault. The retrieval tools above shipped that way — used daily on the author's own vault first, then genericised. The in-shadow and planned items are still proving out: a shadow window to catch false alarms, an adversarial security review on anything that can touch files, and a plain honest status until it clears. The rule holds hardest for the file-changing pieces — those carry the highest bar of all.
